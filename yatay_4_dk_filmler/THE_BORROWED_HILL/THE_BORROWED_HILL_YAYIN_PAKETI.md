A hill on Mars gets smaller for four minutes. The ground it loses ends up two metres
thick on top of a buried steel shelter a hundred metres away.

Everything here is built by machines before anyone arrives: the pad is stripped out of
an ancient ripple field, a trench is cut, fourteen arch ribs go up, the shell is closed,

## PINNED COMMENT

```
The idea behind this is in-situ resource utilisation: the heaviest thing a shelter on
Mars needs is mass over the top of it, and mass is the one thing you cannot afford to
fly there. So you dig it up on site. A buried arch is used because a pressure vessel
cannot have corners — internal pressure tears them — and because a trench plus a cover
is far cheaper in imported mass than a free-standing pressure hull.

The volume in this film balances: the hill is modelled as a 34 × 18 × 16 metre prism,
4,896 cubic metres. 4,724 of those go into the cover, the flanks, the roads, the pad,
the trench backfill and the tank berm. What is left is a three-metre stub. Nothing
arrives from off-screen.
```
